In brief · 30 seconds

What is happening?

🌳

What

At Saidongar in Karjat taluka (Raigad), about 15 km south-west of Bhimashankar sanctuary, the Forest Department has permitted the felling of 32,490 trees on 233 ha of forest land for a 3,000 MW pumped-storage power project. About 1,800–2,700 had been cut before felling was paused on 26–27 Oct.

💧

Why it matters

The project's own environmental report records leopards, sloth bears, pangolins and sambar here. This forest is the catchment of the Pej, a tributary of the Ulhas, and it holds landslide-prone Sahyadri slopes together. Adivasi hamlets live in and around it.

⚖️

The dispute

Government and company: the site is outside the sanctuary, all clearances are in place, the project is green, and 90,000 trees will be planted. Citizens: Sahyadri forest doesn't stop at a line — publish the studies and hear the people first. Some groups say they are approaching the National Green Tribunal.

Chapter 01

Where the Bhima begins

▶ Aerial: “Bhimashankar Drone Video” by kelvin santarita, YouTube

High on the crest of the Western Ghats, where the Deccan plateau suddenly falls away to the Konkan coast, there is a forest that makes its own weather.

For four months of monsoon, clouds sit inside the trees. Moss and orchids cover every branch. When the clouds lift, the Bhima river trickles out of these hills and begins a ~860-km journey east to the Krishna.

To protect this place, and the giant squirrel that lives in its canopy, the Government of Maharashtra notified the Bhimashankar Wildlife Sanctuary in 1985 (state notification of 16 September 1985). It spreads across Ambegaon and Khed talukas of Pune district, reaching into Thane and Raigad.

At its heart stands the Bhimashankar temple, one of the twelve Jyotirlingas, so this forest was sacred to millions long before it was a "sanctuary" on a map.

Chapter 02

The reason it exists: Shekru

Shekru · Maharashtra's state animal

tap to close
Tap the dotsTisha Mukherjee · CC BY-SA 4.0

The Indian giant squirrel (Ratufa indica; the local form is often called elphinstonei) is a squirrel the size of a cat, nearly a metre from nose to the tip of its tail, rust and black and cream. It is Maharashtra's state animal, and this sanctuary was created for it.

A Shekru almost never touches the ground. It builds several leafy nests high in the canopy and leaps up to six metres between crowns. Its whole world is a continuous roof of trees.

Cut a gap in that roof and you don't just lose trees. You cut the squirrel's road.

Chapter 06

Kept by its people

The forest is not empty. Mahadeo Koli and Katkari Adivasi families have lived in and around it for generations.

Their devrai, or sacred groves, are patches no one cuts because they belong to the deity. Fourteen of them survive inside the sanctuary, some holding the oldest trees in the landscape.

Women's self-help groups collect and sell Bhimashankar honey; villages like Yelavali run community eco-tourism for trekkers. The Katkari are recognised as a Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Group. Decisions about this forest are decisions about their lives.

Chapter 07 · Understand the issue

What exactly is the issue?

Slogans aside, in five steps: where, what, how much, when, and your questions.

1

Where is it?

Bhimashankar sanctuary sits on the Sahyadri crest (Pune district). About 15 km to its south-west, the same escarpment drops into Karjat taluka of Raigad. The project site is on these slopes and on the Dhak plateau above them, around the villages of Pali, Potal, Saidongar, Ambot, Dhak and Bhaliwadi, about 10 km east of Karjat town.

The Pej, a small seasonal river that rises on this plateau, flows north-west and joins the Ulhas near Bhivpuri. The Ulhas flows on past Badlapur, Ulhasnagar and Kalyan, a drinking-water source for millions.

Satellite image: Esri World Imagery. Rivers and sanctuary boundary: OpenStreetMap. Project location from the reservoir coordinates in official documents.

2

What is the project?

The project is a giant "water battery". In the day, when solar power is plentiful, water is pumped from a lower dam up to a lake on the hilltop. In the evening, when demand peaks, the same water is let down through turbines to make power.

Official project documents place the upper reservoir on the Dhak plateau and the lower reservoir behind a ~59 m dam across the Pej. The plateau goes under water, and the river is dammed.

Storage like this matters for clean energy. The question is not the technology, it's the location. Felling Western Ghats reserved forest for green energy is a contradiction worth questioning.

5

Your questions

Is the project inside the sanctuary?

No. Official records put it about 15.5 km from the sanctuary and outside its eco-sensitive zone. But it is in the Western Ghats, on the same escarpment, and the project's own environmental report records leopards, sloth bears, pangolins and four-horned antelope here. Our concern is the Sahyadri forest itself, not just the sanctuary line.

Isn't pumped storage green energy?

Yes, it helps store solar and wind power, though about a quarter of the energy is lost in each cycle. Such plants can also use existing reservoirs, or "closed-loop" designs that don't dam a river. This project was in fact first proposed as closed-loop, and later redesigned to dam the Pej.

Won't new planting make up for it?

A sapling is not a forest. A hundred-year-old tree with its moss, orchids, fungi, nests and soil life takes decades, sometimes centuries, to come back. For this project, the compensatory land is about 150 km away in Patan taluka, Satara district, and it is already forested.

Is the 32,490 figure real?

Yes. The Forest Department confirms permission to fell 32,490 trees (14 Oct 2026). The company's own draft environmental report estimated about 48,000. Activists claim the real number is higher, but that is unverified. That gap is why we ask: publish the full tree-enumeration list.

Why the landslide risk?

In heavy rain, Sahyadri slopes are held together by tree roots. Disasters like Malin (2014) and Irshalwadi (2023) happened in this same range. Tunnel blasting and bare slopes can raise that risk, hence the call for an independent study.

Is this the only project here?

No. A second unit, Saidongar-2 (Maval), would share the same lower reservoir, and Tata Power's 1,800 MW Shirawta project is about 6 km away. That's why we ask for a cumulative-impact study.

What do the government and company say?

The government and Torrent say the project has every required clearance; that much of the plateau is scrub and grass with sparse trees (per the environmental report); that downstream drinking and irrigation water won't be affected (per a state hydrology certificate); and that it will avoid millions of tonnes of CO₂ a year compared with coal. Activists say the forest holds trees 200–300 years old. Only an independent survey can settle it.

Chapter 08 · The cut

Now, watch the forest.

This is a model of a forest plateau like the one at Saidongar, in Karjat taluka of Raigad district, about 15 km south-west of Bhimashankar sanctuary. Keep scrolling.

3,000 MW

Torrent Power is building a pumped-storage hydroelectric project here, estimated at ₹13,505 crore. Two reservoirs, tunnels through the hill, and water pumped up and let down to store power.

32,490 trees

Permission has been granted to fell 32,490 trees on 233 hectares (about 575 acres) of forest land. That's roughly 326 football pitches.

Water

The site lies in the valley of the Pej river, a tributary of the Ulhas, which supplies drinking water to towns downstream. Activists warn of water scarcity; the project cites a state hydrology certificate saying downstream users won't be affected.

Hillsides

Tunnel blasting in these hills worries locals who remember Malin (2014) and Irshalwadi (2023), villages buried by landslides in the Sahyadri.

A sapling is not a forest

Compensatory plantation has been promised. But a planted sapling is not a grown forest, with the soil, undergrowth, nests and canopy that took decades or centuries to form.

Chapter 09

What each side says

Facts matter more than slogans. Here's what has been reported, 24–28 October 2026.

Concerned citizens & opposition

  • MLA Rohit Pawar alleged that over 32,000 trees across 233 ha in the Saidongar forest near the sanctuary are being felled. He says he isn't against the project, only its location.
  • The Ulhas River Conservation Action Committee and the Hutatma Dilip Kapote Foundation say they are approaching the National Green Tribunal to stop the felling.
  • They demand public release of the EIA, wildlife-impact study and rehabilitation details, plus a joint study of landslide and water risk.
  • Activists say the public hearing was held outside the affected villages and announced late, and that gram sabhas passed resolutions against the project. When the clearance was recommended, the Forest Rights Act consent of Dhak's gram sabha was still pending.
  • Opposition leaders Aaditya and Raj Thackeray visited the site; within the ruling alliance, Sudhir Mungantiwar, Omraje Nimbalkar and NCP spokesperson Suraj Chavan also questioned the felling.
vs

Forest Department & State

  • The Conservator of Forests, Pune, said no illegal felling is taking place inside the sanctuary.
  • The department says the felling is legal, follows the approved forest-land diversion, and is in the Pali–Dhak area about 16 km from the sanctuary; 1,825 of 32,490 trees had been cut by 26 Oct.
  • On 25 Oct the Chief Minister called the project eco-friendly, said 90,000 trees will be planted, and accused opponents of a "fake narrative".
  • Torrent says all components are outside the sanctuary and its eco-sensitive zone, and that 253 ha of compensatory land has been handed over. Energy minister Meghna Bordikar and MP Ujjwal Nikam defended the project.
  • The Adani Group denied any link, saying it has no project in the area.

Our view: a forest doesn't stop at a boundary line. The sanctuary, the Saidongar plateau and its wildlife, rivers and hillsides are one Sahyadri landscape. Before a single more tree falls, the full studies should be public, the combined impact of all projects here should be assessed, and the people living there should be heard.

The petition

We ask the Government of Maharashtra to:

  1. Keep the current pause on felling at Saidongar until the studies are public and any legal challenge, including before the National Green Tribunal, is heard.
  2. Publish the full EIA, the tree-enumeration list, the approved Wildlife Conservation Plan, the forest-clearance orders, and the gram-sabha consents under the Forest Rights Act.
  3. Order an independent landslide, water-security and cumulative-impact study covering every pumped-storage project in the Pej–Ulhas valley.
  4. Consult gram sabhas and Adivasi communities, as the Forest Rights Act requires.
  5. Consider alternative sites or a closed-loop design that don't sacrifice Western Ghats reserved forest.
